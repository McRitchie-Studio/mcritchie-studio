# Audience and avatar — who you are talking to and how he decides

**Kind:** framework · **Sources:** 16 videos (`3yAiVjcImQ4`, `reisEL_D7xc`,
`dMZ-n2KSlxE`, `JOfsujnXxCg`, `Jmkq5RLjm0U`, `HVOubeXUcx0`, `BYpTRiRqS1Y`,
`5JLqmQlGG0U`, `Wr6n_zNKvMk`, `MH-IMJxbUY4`, `j2TZMFkj71Q`, `i7bLRKwKSms`,
`-j8_YCWZ05Q`, `Mst4hreQYl0`, `o64cI6tebnU`, `mr4Pw66_490`) ·
**Contrarian:** in parts, marked below · **Fit:** all three

## The claim

You pick one avatar, you describe them by what they *did* and not only by what
they look like, you write to where their awareness actually is — and then you
stop guessing, because the audience will tell you what to make if you ask them
the right question.

## The mechanism

### Do not average your avatars

**The arithmetic** (`3yAiVjcImQ4`): with avatars A, B and C, an A client can pay
**10 times** what a C client pays. Most operators average them and charge 2x,
which caps margin and therefore caps how far the business can scale. His
correction is that there are still plenty of A's — serve them first. That best
customer *is* the ideal customer profile.

He reached the diagnosis from the close rate: the operator said yes to everyone,
closed at an exceptionally high rate, and was therefore not capturing the profit
he should have.

The sequencing rule that follows is **1-1-1** (`3yAiVjcImQ4`): one product, one
avatar, one channel until a million a year. The same rule appears from a second
video as a diagnosis rather than a slogan (`Wr6n_zNKvMk`) — an owner feeling
spread thin was running two avatars and five acquisition channels at the stage
where one of each is right, and the prescription shut off paid ads, cold email
and letters entirely.

The corollary, from a different domain, is the same shape: **design for the
extremes, not the average** (`-j8_YCWZ05Q`) — build one busy path and one deep
path, because the average member does not exist and the two ends churn for
opposite reasons.

### Demographics, quantifiables, behaviors

Three buckets for segmenting traffic (`BYpTRiRqS1Y`):

| Bucket | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Demographics** | What they look like |
| **Quantifiables** | The measurable state of their business — size, revenue |
| **Behaviors** | What the ones who **activated and stuck** did that the lookalikes who failed did not |

The first two give you a head start on segmenting traffic. The third has to be
reverse-engineered out of cohort history — and that behavior set becomes the
onboarding you build. It needs enough history to compare stickers against
leavers, so it is not available on day one.

### The five levels of awareness

Borrowed from Eugene Schwartz (`reisEL_D7xc`). Outward from the sale:

1. **Most aware** — past and existing customers; an email converts for near zero
2. **Product aware** — shopping, knows the options
3. **Solution aware** — knows the category exists, not who provides it
4. **Problem aware** — where open-ended questions live: *having trouble sleeping?*
5. **Unaware** — does not know the problem exists

**Each step outward demands an order of magnitude better ad.** The diagnostic
use: when spend will not go past $2,000 or $10,000 or $100,000 a day, the market
is usually big enough and the ads are simply not good enough to reach the next
tranche. His largest portfolio company, roughly $100M a year, markets to the
completely unaware.

The scaling application (`j2TZMFkj71Q`): if ROAS collapses when you raise spend,
it is because every ad speaks to product-aware or offer-aware people. The fix is
**pain-based hooks** — *are you bored, you don't know what to do* — written by
whoever knows the pains inside out. Bottom-funnel offer messaging keeps its
place; the pain hooks are what open the targeting.

The hook-level version is the **call out** (`reisEL_D7xc`, contrarian): drop a
tray of dishes and everyone looks; a child yells *mom* and the moms look; someone
says your name and only you look. All three get attention. Aim as specific as
possible to get the right people, broad enough to get many of them. He flags
that going broader often gets *fewer* qualified leads than going specific, which
surprises people.

### The content is the targeting

**Contrarian, and stated in two separate videos** (`Jmkq5RLjm0U`,
`dMZ-n2KSlxE`). You no longer target an audience — the algorithm reads your
subject matter, your background and your clothing, and shows the piece to people
with a history of watching similar things. So to attract an avatar, make content
*for* that avatar. He concedes it sounds obvious and says nobody does it.

The counter-example does the work: a grandma slapping him gets views, but gets
shown to people interested in humour, which is a lot of people and almost none
of them your customers. The piano-repair guy getting a thousand views a video
has a room of a thousand people who all want their piano fixed — an opportunity,
not a failure.

The framing around it: **social media is becoming interest media**
(`dMZ-n2KSlxE`) — distribution sorted by demonstrated interest rather than by
social graph, so make the topics what your *buyer* finds interesting rather than
what is broadly social.

The paid-side mechanic is the same idea with a different handle: **put the
segment you want in the creative** (`MH-IMJxbUY4`). The platform serves your ad
to people who look like the people *in* it, because likeness is one of the
strongest predictors of conversion. He flags this one as a bet with no informed
data — an inference from how conversion optimization behaves, not a platform
fact. His evidence for likeness beating topic: his own audience is **89% male**
while Leila's is about **54% female**, and they talk about almost exactly the
same things (`dMZ-n2KSlxE`).

And the counter-move to any "widen the targeting to lower costs" proposal:
**broadening the funnel buys trash** (`i7bLRKwKSms`, contrarian). Owners open the
top of the funnel, get way more and way more trash, then conclude the customers
suck. His diagnosis is an unwillingness to spend more for a better customer.

### Say what only you can say, show what only you can show

Two videos, the same framework (`reisEL_D7xc`, `JOfsujnXxCg`). Market from what
you **did** — *how I* — not from instructions — *how to* — because only your own
actions are unmatchable.

The split that makes it usable early: proof comes from **outcomes** or from
**labour**. Outcomes are accomplishments. Labour is *35,000 posts, independent of
the outcome* — and labour is available to someone with no results yet. Early on
you claim the work; once the outcome exists, the outcome version is a shorter
headline. It also dodges other people's judgment, because you are not telling
them what to do.

Its structural partner is **assume nothing** (`Jmkq5RLjm0U`, `HVOubeXUcx0`,
`JOfsujnXxCg`): title for strangers, introduce yourself every time, say why they
should listen every time, and fully explain or kill every inside joke — because
if you tell an inside joke and someone does not get it, they are on the outside.
Way more people do not know you than know you.

### How the audience tells you what to make

The corpus is unusually concrete here, and the instruction is always to **cross
the preference question with a qualification question**:

- **Self-segment in the comments** (`dMZ-n2KSlxE`) — *tell me in the comments if
  you like this style, and if you do, tell me whether you're a business owner*.
  He enumerates
  all four cells, because a bare "do you like this" cannot tell you whether the
  people who like it are the people who buy.
- **Ask your audience the name** (`Mst4hreQYl0`) — three tiers by budget: paid
  headline tests, a story poll prefaced by a disqualifier (*if you are not a
  business owner please do not answer*), or texting people directly. Measure not
  only which name draws the most leads but which draws the highest quality ones.
- **Ask your real customers, in person** (`Jmkq5RLjm0U`) — his replacement signal
  is asking owners who fly in what their favourite format is, and watching for
  texts from owners he respects.
- **Are you making content for your team or your avatar?** (`JOfsujnXxCg`,
  contrarian) — he asked his team what to make, they suggested videos, and he
  made videos for them. His team was not his buyer.

### Size the market before you set a target

**The 9% map** (`dMZ-n2KSlxE`): about 9% of Americans own a business, roughly
32-33 million; 95% of those are under $1M, leaving about **1.5 million** above
it; above $10M is about 1 in 250, above $100M roughly 1 in 3,000. His conclusion:
100,000 views on a video made for that owner is crushing it, and comparing it to
a 100-million-view entertainment video makes no sense — different games. The
instruction is to build the equivalent map for your own category **before**
setting a view target.

### When your avatar stops being the customer

One inversion worth carrying (`o64cI6tebnU`): when headcount is the constraint,
**salespeople are your new avatar**. Point the marketing function at recruiting
and treat reps as the customer you now have to acquire — more reps means volume,
volume means reliability, reliability means you can forecast, and forecasting
lets you cut the cost basis.

## When it applies

- **1-1-1** is a sequencing rule below roughly a million a year, not a permanent
  ban on product lines. He marks it contrarian himself.
- **Behaviors** needs cohort history. Without it you have two buckets, not three.
- **The awareness ladder** is a diagnostic for a *hard ceiling on spend*, and a
  standing warning against sending warm messaging to cold audiences.
- **The content-is-targeting** items are aimed at educators and service
  businesses, not entertainers, and the consequence he draws is to stop judging
  content by views and start judging it by *who showed up*.
- **Likeness in the creative** is explicitly his inference, not a measured fact.

## Two things the corpus does not say

**Frustrated Fred and Wealthy William is not an avatar pair.** It is worth
saying plainly, because the names read like customer segments. In the corpus
(`mr4Pw66_490`) they are two **owners** with identical revenue and profit — $10M
top line, $2M bottom — who differ ~20x in wealth created because only one built
a business that runs without him. Fred works 80 hours and banks about $500,000 a
year after tax; William's trades at 6x profit, so $500,000 of new profit adds
$3M of enterprise value. The multiple only exists if the business creates value
without the owner. Use it as an **owner** diagnostic, or as an avatar frame only
where your buyer is a business owner and the question is which of the two he is.

**"You are the niche" is for personal brands only** (`5JLqmQlGG0U`). A personal
brand's niche is the unique combination *and proportion* of one person's genuine
interests — Joe Rogan's niche is none of his topics and all of them. He draws
the distinction sharply himself: a **business** brand should be built around the
product and the avatar who uses it. The companion measure is bond strength — how
many of your declared interests a given follower shares — which means narrowing
the interest set narrows the audience but deepens the bond.

## Where the corpus disagrees with itself

**Are the comments a signal or a trap?** Both, from the same man, about the same
$10-million-a-year friend.

- `JOfsujnXxCg` — *listen to them*. He calls it his own failure: *"I should have
  seen that more business owners were telling us in the comments what they
  wanted and honestly I just wasn't listening."* The correction produced 25% more
  comments per view.
- `HVOubeXUcx0` and `Jmkq5RLjm0U` — *they led him astray*. Commenters praised the
  newer styles while the buyer he could help most had stopped watching. His
  conclusion: comments came from people who were not the who he was serving.

Rex's reading: the reconcilable version is that comment **volume** is not the
signal and comment **composition** is. Both stories are solved by the
self-segment play above — ask for the qualification alongside the preference,
and the two lessons stop fighting.

## One number to check before it leaves the building

The figure **9%** carries two different statistics in the corpus: 9% of
Americans own a business (`dMZ-n2KSlxE`, and again inside the value-equation
item at `reisEL_D7xc`) and 9% of Americans are millionaires (`BYpTRiRqS1Y`).
Both are independently plausible, and the agreement across two videos on the
business-owner figure is real corroboration — but do not let the coincidence
turn into one remembered statistic.

## Rex's read for our three clients

- **industries** — the avatar is not "anyone who needs welding." Rank last
  year's jobs by margin, find the A's, and write the call out as a flat identity
  statement a Denver-metro plant manager reads as *this is for me*. The shop's
  proof is labour and finished work, which is exactly what it can show and
  nobody else can.
- **turf-monster** — we post to a general sports audience and cannot say who
  showed up. Run the four-cell self-segment ask on the next post (*do you like
  this — and do you enter contests?*), and size the real market before calling
  a view count good or bad.
- **operator-brand** — a studio run by agents is the `5JLqmQlGG0U` case only if
  the brand is a person; if it is the studio, build around the product and the
  avatar who uses it. Until outcomes exist, claim the labour: the ship log is
  proof nobody else can show.
