# Scaling order — what you do first, and what breaks next

**Kind:** framework · **Sources:** 9 videos (`reisEL_D7xc`, `OQf2Ba-Lp_4`,
`0EqJD2o-Mnk`, `BYpTRiRqS1Y`, `N5MExtki_VI`, `MH-IMJxbUY4`, `j2TZMFkj71Q`,
`B-ogfFiQpXg`, `KhFlD54nQrY`) ·
**Contrarian:** in parts, marked below · **Fit:** all three

## The claim

Growth is not a goal you set. It is what happens after you remove the one thing
currently limiting the business — so the work is always **find the constraint,
fix that, push volume until you hit the next one**. And the order inside that
loop is nearly always *more* before *better* before *new*.

## The mechanism

### More, Better, New — and the arithmetic for switching

**Contrarian** (`reisEL_D7xc`). Scale in that order: do **more** of what works,
then do it **better**, and try **new** only when nothing else is left. Most
entrepreneurs invert it and spend 80% of their time on new when they should
spend 90% on more and better.

**The switch point has arithmetic.** Move from *more* to *better* when the
return on one increment of better exceeds one increment of more:

| State | One more unit | What that is worth |
|---|---|---|
| 1 salesperson | → 2 | **+100%** for that unit of effort |
| 10 salespeople | → 11 | **+10%** for the same effort |

At ten, it is cheaper to lift team-wide throughput 20-25% with a headline split
test or a CTA change than to add the eleventh person. **New comes last** because
*the cost of change is guaranteed and the return is not*.

The one exception he names: when literally nothing works, everything is new —
you keep experimenting until one thing works, then you unlearn experimentation.

The compressed forms he uses on himself: *nail it, then scale it*, and the flat
comparison that a hundred repetitions beat a hundred things done once
(`reisEL_D7xc`).

### Supply constrained or demand constrained — ask before you prescribe

**This is the first question on any business** (`OQf2Ba-Lp_4`): *if we doubled
the lead flow tomorrow, could you handle it?*

- **Yes** → demand constrained. Go to work on marketing.
- **No** → supply constrained. Build resources and infrastructure first.

His warning is that owners routinely try to fix a problem whose solution makes
their real problem worse. A second video opens on the same question in different
words — *if you double the amount of customers, does it break anything?* — and
notes that a *no* scopes the whole session to offer and traffic and nothing else
(`j2TZMFkj71Q`).

**Demand constrained → exactly three moves** (`MH-IMJxbUY4`):

```
demand problem
├── new channel
├── lower CAC ──┬── conversion rate optimization
│               ├── better creative
│               └── better offer
└── spend more
```

When an owner hits a wall each time they raise spend, the answer lives in the
**lower CAC** branch — more budget into the same creative is what produced the
wall.

**Supply constrained → exactly four moves** (`N5MExtki_VI`), and each commits
you to a different company:

| Move | The business you become |
|---|---|
| Raise the price | A **branding** business — holding supply fixed is the only way price keeps rising |
| Change the delivery ratio (1:1 → 1:4 → 1:many) | Kicks the can, in a good way — the operator keeps improving |
| Productize delivery | A **marketing and sales** business — acquisition carries everything |
| Hire people to deliver | A **recruiting, training and culture** business |

Pick by which problem you would rather own, and by cash position. He ruled out
hiring (cash constrained), pricing (already struggled to sell at current price)
and productizing (already built one that would not sell at the premium).

### Finding the constraint

Three phrasings of the same move, and the third is the one that catches the hard
cases:

- **What would stop me from 10x-ing the business right now?** (`reisEL_D7xc`) —
  if the answer is nothing, 10x it now. If something would break, that is the
  constraint: isolate it, fix it, push volume until the next bottleneck.
- **What breaks when we do more?** (`0EqJD2o-Mnk`) — then *what stops you from
  fixing that*, then press the modal verbs: *have to or choose to*, *can't or
  won't*.
- **The multi-step constraint chain** (`0EqJD2o-Mnk`) — *if it were one thing,
  you probably would have already seen it*, so the real fix is usually second or
  third order. His worked chain for an electrical contractor at $1.6M: demand is
  fine → the constraint is people → to hire redundancy you need cash flow → to
  get cash flow you raise price → to raise price you need a better offer → the
  offer improves by making the service faster and guaranteeing it. The visible
  problem was hiring; the fix was the offer.

And once you have it: **one or two big levers** (`N5MExtki_VI`). Every business
has many things it could improve, but usually one or two are the actual levers.
Everything else gets deliberately parked.

### The scaling roadmap — stages keyed to headcount

Ten stages from zero to $100M, built from 200+ hours of studying businesses,
broken across eight functions, each with its constraint, its symptoms, and the
steps to graduate (`OQf2Ba-Lp_4`). The worked example: **10-19 people is stage
five, "productize"**, whose symptoms are customers having nothing else to buy
and churning, qualified leads getting too expensive and capping ad spend — and
whose work is sales materials, pricing, sales process and a CS playbook. The
underlying claim is that **businesses behave in patterns**, so the stage
predicts the problem. He gives it away at acquisition.com/roadmap.

The companion move (`B-ogfFiQpXg`): **name the constraint that comes next**. At
16 staff and $3M he placed a practice between productize and optimize and named
what was coming — quality degrading as volume rises and newer staff arrive,
sales training becoming the bottleneck once events scale, and clear cohorts
appearing around 1,500 customers including a segment willing to pay far more.
None of it urgent; all of it around the corner.

There is also a back-of-napkin version for what to **add** next
(`reisEL_D7xc`) — note you are still on **one avatar** well past eight figures:

| Stage | Avatar | Channel | Product |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 → six figures | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| six → seven figures | 1 | 1 | 1, *consistently* |
| seven → eight figures | 1 | 1 | **2** (add an upsell) |
| multiple eight figures | 1 | **2** | 2, consistently |

Businesses stall where they skipped a step. He notes exceptions exist and says
to break it knowingly.

### Conquer a city before a country

**Contrarian** (`BYpTRiRqS1Y`). Applied to an operator doing ~$4M a year who
wanted a national coaching stage: to conquer the world you first conquer a
country, and before that a city — and he has not conquered a city. He tells him
he could realistically reach $100M inside a single metro pair, so **the national
vision is not ambition, it is avoidance**. Fortify the base; ignore the national
thing. When armies overexpand, they collapse.

The condition: this stops applying once the local market is genuinely saturated.

### Expand the 20% to 100%

Learned from Vista Equity's head of pricing and packaging (`reisEL_D7xc`), whose
stated edge was knowing an acquired company's customers better than the company
did. The operation: order customers by who pays the most and is worth the most,
isolate the **20% doing 80% of revenue**, look at which channels and offers
those people came through, **stop the other 80%**, and expand the 20% to be the
whole business — same size company, 5x the revenue and more on profit.

To run it yourself, cluster your top 20% on psychographics, geographics,
demographics and **actions** — what they did to get the result — eliminating
variables until two or three remain. Then market only to those, and tell sales
to sell only those. It needs enough customer history to rank, and the mix takes
a year or two to shift.

Its natural sequel is **five ways to expand a market** (`reisEL_D7xc`,
contrarian): up market, down market, adjacent, **narrower**, or broader — and
more than half the portfolio companies that ran the exercise went **narrower**.
Only consider expansion after you are genuinely the best in the current niche;
otherwise the answer is get better.

### What to do before you pour money in

- **Fix the funnel before you raise spend** (`OQf2Ba-Lp_4`, contrarian). His
  stated order: price raise → pay debt → change the funnel → fix the ads →
  increase ad spend → retargeting and branded search → outbound events. The
  reason for the order is that fixing the landing page may already double or
  triple lead flow at current spend, at which point the owner discovers he does
  not have enough technicians. Raising spend first buys that problem at a higher
  price.
- **Do not break what already works** (`j2TZMFkj71Q`). On a profitable model,
  sequence the safe change first and run the bold one as a split test at an
  identical price point so everything else stays constant. The verdict criterion
  is whether anything will get in the way of scaling, not whether the plan is
  clever.
- **Grow the pyramid, then skim it with ads** (`BYpTRiRqS1Y`). Organic grows the
  **base** of the audience pyramid so the monetized percentage stays the same
  and the dollar figure rises; ads leave the base alone and move the
  **monetization line down**, converting people further from the top. Short
  term, doubling is pulling the ad lever; on a ten-year horizon you run both.

### His rough sizing of each lever

Stated live while prioritizing one local service business (`OQf2Ba-Lp_4`) —
**these are estimates he gives on the spot, not measured results**, and he
labels them as such:

| Lever | Expected effect |
|---|---|
| 10% price raise | ~25% more net profit |
| Landing page + redirecting site buttons | 2x or more |
| Fixing ads, running organic winners | 2-3x |
| Increased ad spend | 1:1 with everything downstream, if ROAS holds |
| Retargeting + branded search | 10%, conservatively, maybe 20% |
| Reactivation emails | 20-30%, and compounds |
| Outbound events | uncapped |

Treat as order-of-magnitude sizing for **prioritization**, not as a forecast.

## When it applies

- **More/Better/New** applies once something is working. With nothing working,
  everything is new.
- **The supply/demand question** is asked *before* prescribing anything. It
  determines whether marketing work is the right work at all.
- **The four supply moves** are chosen by which problem you would rather own and
  by cash position — not by which is cheapest.
- **Conquer a city** does not apply to a saturated local market.
- **Expand the 20%** needs enough history to rank customers.

## Where the corpus disagrees with itself

**Do more of what works, or deliberately hold volume down?**

- `0EqJD2o-Mnk` (contrarian) — **do way more of the thing you already do**.
  A junk-removal owner at $1M got customers from in-person networking; rather
  than start email or LinkedIn, the prescription was four hours a day finding
  local events, going from a few a week to several a day. The other channels
  have not been started at all, so saturating the working one is the lowest-risk
  path from $1M to $3M, and the cash funds learning a second channel later.
- `KhFlD54nQrY` (contrarian) — **advertise only enough to keep iterating**. If
  you plan to go big, you want as few people as possible to discover the product
  while it is mediocre: *long-term all you do is you just let lots of people know
  that your food's mediocre*. The trigger to pour gasoline is word of mouth —
  when people cannot help telling everybody they talked to.

Rex's reading: the boundary is **whether the thing that works is the channel or
the product**. Saturate a proven channel; throttle a mediocre product. His own
proof for the second is a company that grew every day with zero marketing before
he would co-own it.

**More first, or better first?** `reisEL_D7xc` says more is almost always the
answer when starting; `OQf2Ba-Lp_4` explicitly sequences conversion fixes ahead
of budget increases. These reconcile through the supply constraint — "more
spend" is the increment that can buy you a delivery problem, while "more of a
free channel" cannot. When the increment of *more* costs money, check capacity
first.

## Rex's read for our three clients

- **industries** — ask the supply question first and expect a *no*: a welding
  shop's constraint is usually the floor, not the phone, which puts us in the
  four supply moves and probably on price. Then conquer a city — the Denver
  metro is nowhere near saturated, and any plan that reaches past it is
  avoidance.
- **turf-monster** — demand constrained and squarely in the *lower CAC* branch,
  but we cannot see which child to pick because nothing is measured. Instrument
  first; the funnel fix almost certainly outranks more posting.
- **operator-brand** — nothing works yet, so we are in the one state where
  everything is new: experiment until one thing works, then stop experimenting
  and do that thing a hundred times. Hold volume down until the story lands, not
  because we are shy but because a mediocre first impression scales too.
