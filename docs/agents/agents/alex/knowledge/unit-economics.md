## The claim

One ratio governs a business: **lifetime gross profit per customer, divided by
what it costs to acquire one.** Not a tactic, not a funnel — a ratio `i7bLRKwKSms`.
If he could know one thing about a business, this is it `reisEL_D7xc`.

The near-universal error is computing **LTV on revenue**. LTV is lifetime gross
**profit**: what you keep after the cost of delivering. Pay $100 for a sandwich
that costs $20 to make and the gross profit is $80; ten purchases is $800 of
LTGP `i7bLRKwKSms`.

## The mechanism

### Compute both numbers off a full year of bookkeeping

He insists the crude, year-long version is **more** accurate than tracking
monthly, because it averages good and bad months `i7bLRKwKSms`.

- **CAC** = last year's ad spend + sales commissions + payroll for anyone whose
  job is to get customers (a videographer, a contractor) ÷ new customers. $100,000
  over 100 customers is a $1,000 CAC. It is *not* cost per lead — cheap leads are
  easy to get and may never become customers.
- **LTGP** = total revenue ÷ total customers, times gross margin. Gross margin is
  total cost of delivery for the year ÷ customers. For a recurring business this
  underestimates, which is the safe direction `reisEL_D7xc`.

> "if you can't go through that exercise with your business you don't understand
> your business well enough"

### The 3:1 rule, and the condition on it

The rule of thumb is **at least 3:1** LTGP to CAC — CAC at most a third of
LTGP `reisEL_D7xc`. You cannot acquire at break-even, because nothing is left to
run the business with. Worked example: $10 meals costing $9 to deliver leave $1
each; five weeks is $50 of LTGP, so CAC must be $15 or less.

**But 3:1 only holds if lead generation, conversion and delivery are all
automated** `i7bLRKwKSms`. Each function with people in it raises the floor by
roughly three:

| Automated functions | Minimum ratio |
|---|---|
| All three (content, ads, one-to-many delivery) | 3:1 |
| Two | 6:1 |
| One | 9:1 |
| None — manual outreach, human sales, human delivery | 12:1 or better |

His reasons for the padding: CAC rises as you go colder, management layers add
cost as you scale, later customers are less sold and worth less, and every new
hire performs worst on day one while the cost lands immediately.

### What the ranges mean

| Range | Reading |
|---|---|
| Below 1:1 | You are losing money, or your attribution is lying. A $3M roofer bought leads at a **$7,500 CAC against a $6,500 customer** `0EqJD2o-Mnk`. But a reported 0.41:1 on a business that is visibly profitable means the **data** is wrong `HGZOxBfnF-E` |
| ~1.4:1 | Skinny. The travel-course business at $6.4M was here, and the ceiling was creative quality, not budget `MH-IMJxbUY4` |
| 3:1 – 12:1 | Viable, depending on automation (above) |
| 5:1 – 10:1 | "Maintenance era." Fine numbers. A 10:1 often falls to 5:1 the moment you scale it `reisEL_D7xc` |
| 20:1 warm → 5:1 mid → 3:1 at scale | Expected decay as you buy colder audiences — **watermelon seed density**. Fewer seeds per slice as you go down, same price per eyeball. Accept it: absolute return rises with the bigger tranche `reisEL_D7xc` |
| 30:1 – 200:1 | "Crazy money." Short punctuated windows that do not last. $1,000/day at 100:1 suggests $100,000/day at 5:1 is reachable `reisEL_D7xc` |

**Read reported ratios with suspicion, including his own.** He states his gym
Facebook ads at 30:1 and Gym Launch at 100:1, then adds the footnote himself:
much of the $10M was word of mouth, and he does not know what he would have made
at zero ad spend `KhFlD54nQrY`. Word of mouth is *inside* a reported ROAS, not
outside it.

### Payback — the 30-day window

Beyond the ratio, get the CAC back inside **30 days** `i7bLRKwKSms` `reisEL_D7xc`.
Two reasons, both about cash: 30 days is about as long as a small business can
float, and it is the interest-free window on a credit card. He notes 99% of
business owners have no investors beyond themselves.

The explicit form: **cash collected in the first cycle must exceed fully loaded
cost of delivery plus CAC** `Wr6n_zNKvMk`. On a residential trash business that
meant naming every dollar out the door — $42 of labour and dump fees, $50 sales
commission, $116 for the bin and freight — and designing the offer to collect
more than $208 at the door. That business was already at 20:1 LTV to CAC and
still losing $151,000 a year. **Ratio health and cash health are different
questions.**

The same lever won him the gym market: the industry sold a $21 trial, he
collected about $1,000 in the first 30 days `i7bLRKwKSms`.

> "the business that can make more money from its customer than its competitors
> wins"

### Four ways to lower CAC `i7bLRKwKSms`

1. **Offer** — make the thing more compelling.
2. **Creative** — more ads, better hooks, remake your winners.
3. **Conversion rate** — split-test pages, follow-up, phone scripting; train the team.
4. **CPMs** — advertise where eyeballs cost less.

Two caps on that list, from him. First, CAC reduction is competing on *methods*,
which is copyable. Second, and stronger: **"we need cheaper leads" is usually the
wrong request** — the real problem is that they do not make enough per customer,
because they are mispriced or the thing is not good enough `KhFlD54nQrY`. And
**CAC only goes up** anyway: CPMs rise, competitors arrive, and scaling pushes
you into colder audiences `i7bLRKwKSms`.

Note also that every added funnel step costs about **half** the traffic
`OQf2Ba-Lp_4`. Collapsing a redundant step is CAC reduction that costs nothing.

### Seven ways to raise LTGP `i7bLRKwKSms`

Raise price · decrease cost · upsell · downsell · financing · change payment
terms · cross-sell.

Financing and terms are specifically **cash-forward** levers. The dangerous one
is the **downsell**: selling a cheap thing to someone who would have bought the
expensive thing loses money. It only helps if it converts people who would not
otherwise buy.

### Price is the strongest lever, and here is the arithmetic

| Move | Effect | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Raise price 10% | ~25% more net profit; breakeven holds as long as close rate stays above 65% | `OQf2Ba-Lp_4` |
| Close rate at or above 80% | You are underpriced. He penciled a raise on hearing 82% | `OQf2Ba-Lp_4` |
| Raise 20% every five customers | After the first 10-20 case-study customers. Stop when **conversion rate × price** starts falling — that is the sweet spot | `reisEL_D7xc` |
| Shrink the first payment | Conversion tracks the **first payment**, not the total. $5,000 → $25-30K held because cash up front went $5,000 → $9,000 | `N5MExtki_VI` |
| Stop averaging your avatars | An A client can pay 10x a C client; pricing for the blend caps both margin and scale | `3yAiVjcImQ4` |
| Chase gross margin, not order value | Same average order, double or triple the margin, beats winning a price-shopping segment | `HGZOxBfnF-E` |
| Margin × frequency | A $1,500 repair at 64% returns ~$1,100 against ~$7,000 on a $18-25K replacement — but sells three times as often | `o64cI6tebnU` |

The test for whether you have pricing power at all is Buffett's, which he quotes:
if you need a prayer session before raising the price 10%, you have a terrible
business `VQM3DrnVTcs`.

**Brand is the engine under the price lever, and it is measurable.** Brand pays
out as higher click-through, higher repurchase and a price premium — which map
onto lower CAC, higher LTV and higher gross profit `qsXxckCbci0`. Sized: roughly
12x the price on an identical commodity and about six times the click-through
`Jmkq5RLjm0U` `VQM3DrnVTcs`. Sized as ROI: sell a million units at $3 above market
and the brand bought $3M of excess; a $300,000 endorsement against that is 10:1
`KhFlD54nQrY`. Without it you are a **smash-and-grab** — buying eyeballs at $10
to make $11 of gross profit, with no moat `qsXxckCbci0`.

### Retention is an LTV lever, and it is bigger than it looks

Halving churn **doubles** LTV: 20% → 10% monthly churn is not a 10% improvement,
customers stay twice as long `-j8_YCWZ05Q`. And the counter-intuitive fix —
narrowing to a better avatar deliberately **raises** your CAC, and that is the
price of fixing churn `BYpTRiRqS1Y`.

### Once you are demand constrained, there are exactly three moves `MH-IMJxbUY4`

New channel · lower CAC (→ conversion rate, creative, or offer) · spend more. If
an owner hits a wall every time they raise spend, the answer lives in the
lower-CAC branch, because more budget into the same creative is what built the
wall.

And watch for the self-imposed ceiling, which looks like economics and is not: a
profitable membership business capped CPA at about $50 "for no good reason" while
recovering it inside the first month, and finished every launch wishing it had
spent more `j2TZMFkj71Q`. The amount you are willing to spend is a decision, not a
market constraint.

## Where the corpus disagrees with itself

**3:1 is stated as a flat rule in one video and as a conditional in another.**
`reisEL_D7xc` gives it plainly — "the rule of thumb is that you want to have at
least three to one." `i7bLRKwKSms` accepts it **only** where lead generation,
conversion and delivery are all automated, and puts a mostly-manual service
business at 12:1 or better. That is a 4x difference in the floor, and it decides
whether a business is viable. Rex uses the conditional version: check the
automation profile before quoting 3:1 at anyone.

Two smaller tensions worth knowing:

- **Lower CAC** is one of three legitimate moves in `MH-IMJxbUY4`, is capped as
  copyable in `i7bLRKwKSms`, and is called the wrong request outright in
  `KhFlD54nQrY`. The boundary is which side of the ratio is actually thin.
- **A healthy ratio is not a healthy business.** `Wr6n_zNKvMk` is 20:1 and losing
  $151,000 a year on cash timing. The ratio and the payback window are two gates,
  not one.

## When it applies

Everywhere, but the ratio answers *whether* to spend, never *where*. It also
cannot be computed on a business without attribution — a reported ratio that
contradicts a profitable P&L is a data defect, and the correct action is neither
more spend nor less `HGZOxBfnF-E`.

Worked shape for a lead magnet, since it is the cheapest place to see the math:
one hour of labour at $25 hard cost, worth $250 retail, converting one in four,
buys a customer for $100. The only question left is whether a customer is worth
more than $100 `Mst4hreQYl0`.

## Rex's read for our three clients

- **industries** — run the back-of-napkin version off last year's books: total
  gross profit ÷ customers, and a CAC that is close to zero because everything
  arrives by word of mouth. That means enormous headroom before any paid channel
  breaks even. But the automation ladder puts this shop at **9:1 or 12:1**, not
  3:1 — human lead gen, human quoting, human delivery — and the fastest lever is
  a price raise, which needs no marketing function at all.
- **turf-monster** — the rare business where 3:1 is genuinely the right floor:
  content and ads generate, the app converts, software delivers. We still cannot
  use it, because the LTGP numerator (entry fee minus prize, rake and chain cost)
  is unmeasured and CAC is unmeasurable while no post links to an entry. Also
  check the 30-day window — escrowed prize money is cash out before it is cash in.
- **operator-brand** — CAC here is hours, not dollars, and there is no LTGP
  because there is no offer. Use this card as a **gate**: we do not buy attention
  until we can name what a customer is worth.
