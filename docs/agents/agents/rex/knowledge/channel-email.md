## The claim

**Email is content you own the distribution of.** He puts it in the content
bucket, not in a channel of its own: one message, many people, free — with one
asymmetry in its favour `OpeN4O5myIg`.

> "When you make a post, the algorithm decides who gets to see it. When you send
> an email, your past performance determines it." `pLhQOYMGa88`

And the strategic frame he keeps returning to:

> "The email is kind of like your 401k. It's your investment account that you put
> money into and then over time it starts to compound." `OpeN4O5myIg`

Ads are the paycheck — you run them every day and get paid back proportionally.
The list those ads build is the investment account. **Email revenue drops
straight to the bottom line** (the lead was already paid for), and because about
95% of businesses trade on a multiple of bottom line, that is a large valuation
effect; list size also does not swing overnight the way ad performance does, so
consistent email revenue smooths volatility `OpeN4O5myIg`.

## What a send is for

Two purposes only, the same two as any content: **increase the likelihood this
person purchases in the future, or get them to purchase** `pLhQOYMGa88`.

The design rule underneath every tactic on this card is **reward every step**:

> "If you want to see why someone does something, look at what happens after they
> do it." `pLhQOYMGa88`

They open because past issues rewarded opening, so the top of the email must
reward this open. They read, so the PS must reward reading. They click, so the
destination must be worth the click. **Four good emails and one useless one can
end the habit** — which is why he would rather extend the gap than send
something bad `pLhQOYMGa88`.

## The mechanism

### Campaign structure — the Mozi Money Minute

His newsletter template, five parts, every time `pLhQOYMGa88`:

| Part | Job |
|---|---|
| **Subject** | Branded and **constant**, so the newsletter's brand earns the open, not that issue's promise |
| **A quote or tweet** | The immediate reward, in one glance, for having opened |
| **The body** | **One tactic only, under ~200 words** — about a minute. Structured as the problem or condition that makes the solution viable → the solution → the how |
| **The CTA** | One, or two if the audiences are genuinely different |
| **The PS** | The second reward: they have now read it, so pay them for reading |

> "My whole goal was, how do I provide value in under 60 seconds." `pLhQOYMGa88`

**The length is not a rule.** He notes Leila sends much longer, story-driven
emails to the highest-revenue segment and they perform well `pLhQOYMGa88`.

**Then lock the format.** Experiment early, and once a format produces more
responses or click-throughs, repeat it unchanged. His analogy is McDonald's:
today's Big Mac is different food from yesterday's, but the customer wants it to
be identical in form and just as good `pLhQOYMGa88`.

A second, visual variant for a category where the result is the appeal — a
twice-weekly nurture rotating **three themes**: a before-and-after, a cool build,
and an FAQ drawn from the 20 questions non-buyers always ask. Structure:
subject → an immediate reward at the top, usually a striking image → the CTA or
FAQ link → a PS with a discount or a joke `HGZOxBfnF-E`.

### The full campaign breakdown — a 7-day challenge to 15% of a list

The one campaign the corpus takes apart end to end `OpeN4O5myIg`. A consumer
business doing almost $10M/month on paid ads, with a **3.3 million** email list
collected over years and used only for transaction follow-up. Ads stopped
performing and the quarterly goal was at risk.

They pulled a component **out of the existing product**, wrapped it as a 7-day
challenge lead magnet, and mailed a **464,000-person sub-segment** — deliberately
~15% of the list, weighted to non-buyers, so a bomb would not burn everyone.

| Step | Number |
|---|---|
| Sent | 464,000 |
| Opened | 18% → 83,000 |
| Clicked | ~5% of openers |
| Registered | 10,000 |
| Bought at $2,000 | **800 (8%)** = $1.6M |
| Ascended to the back end | **20% of buyers** = $800K |
| **Total** | **$2.4M in five days** |

He calls the 8% pretty good and says he is not unhappy with it, against an
audience he describes as slightly warmer than true cold `OpeN4O5myIg`.

**The planning number is per-email, not per-campaign.** $2.4M ÷ 464,000 ≈ **$6
per email address on the list**. Run the play once a quarter and you can forecast
what an unworked list is worth for the year `OpeN4O5myIg`. The contrast he draws:
acquiring a customer for that $2,000 product **through ads cost $1,200**; over
email, once the lead is already on the list, **CAC is $0**, so the whole gross
profit drops to the bottom line.

**Then they did not wait a quarter.** They ran the identical play the next month
against a different sub-segment and did **$2.2M**, with the plan for the
following quarter being the whole list, carrying both sets of learnings
`OpeN4O5myIg`.

> "One of my favorite business strategies: do something that works, do it again,
> do more of it, and ideally do it better." `OpeN4O5myIg`

### The quarterly cleanup — the campaign calendar

Four parts `OpeN4O5myIg`:

1. **Once a quarter**, run every lead you have collected through a fresh
   conversion event. Q1 cleans Q1's leads, Q2 cleans Q2's. One big push, not
   constant effort.
2. **Test on 10-15% of the list first**, weighted toward non-buyers. One, two or
   three test runs before the full send. His reasoning: as soon as you learn what
   you got wrong on the first attempt, you will be grateful you held the rest
   back.
3. **Change the wrapper every time** — the landing page, the subject line, the
   name of the challenge — even though the deliverable is identical, so a
   non-buyer who passed twice still sees something new.
4. **Between campaigns, just provide value**, so you have deposited enough
   goodwill to earn the ask.

Once you have four tested annual promos, they become autopilot and you reuse the
calendar year after year `OpeN4O5myIg`.

The wrapper library from his gym years, all wrapping the same eat-less-move-more
deliverable: a six-week challenge, a 42-day fix, a 42-day transformation, a
six-week booty blast, slim for Santa, lean by Halloween, slim for summer, an
accountability buddy challenge `OpeN4O5myIg`. And **small audiences fatigue
faster** — a local business needs far more variety per cycle than a national one,
because the same people see every promotion `OpeN4O5myIg`.

A parallel calendar device for seasonal categories: **two seasonal pain points
cover a full year** if you run pre / during / post on each. The instruction to a
CMO is a *theme*, not a campaign — all messaging for the next two months is
anti-allergy, across email, paid social and PPC together `OQf2Ba-Lp_4`.

### Cadence — and his own refusal to commit to one

| Rule | Source |
|---|---|
| **~3 emails a week** is the perfect cadence, per the best email marketers he knows. If you have not started, **start at once a month** | `pLhQOYMGa88` |
| **Twice a week** as a nurture cadence for a long-repurchase-cycle category | `HGZOxBfnF-E` |
| **Twice a week to affiliates** during a campaign — one email of usable content, one of their standings — with the expectation set on day one | `60_7PU9JDIw` |
| **2, maximum 3 promotional asks** to a customer list over an *entire campaign* | `60_7PU9JDIw` |
| **3-4 asks a year** to his own list, against ~250 pieces of content a week | `60_7PU9JDIw` |

The general finding: the more often you email, the more you convert, up to the
point where people feel bothered — and **that point moves out if the emails
reliably make the reader money** `pLhQOYMGa88`.

His own unlock was committing to **twice a month** and writing **24 emails in
about half a day** — a year of email off one afternoon `pLhQOYMGa88`.

### Give to ask

> "Every time you make an offer you lose goodwill. So make as many as you can
> while also providing as much value as you can." `OpeN4O5myIg`

Money made correlates with number of offers made; each offer spends goodwill; so
the two must stay in balance. In the $2.4M campaign the balance worked *despite*
thin value deposits, because they had barely asked at all in years — **an unused
list is itself a goodwill reserve** `OpeN4O5myIg`.

His own ratio is extreme: ~250 pieces of content a week, ~12,000 a year, against
**three or four asks** `60_7PU9JDIw`. The bar he sets on an ask is why: ordinary
content might be liked by 80% and thought mid by 20%, but he only asks when he
believes **99% of people will find it worth their time**, because the ask is a
promise that the return exceeds the time spent `60_7PU9JDIw`.

**Two ways to get more at-bats without burning goodwill:**

- **Sell the same offer in a different medium.** Webinar, phone, live in-person
  workshop, email — four asks, one offer, much less fatigue, because people judge
  goodwill by medium `OpeN4O5myIg`.
- **Ask again.** People get busy, leave a cart open, their kid screams and they
  forget. Asking a second time is fine once you have earned the right
  `OpeN4O5myIg`.

### List building

- **A lead magnet is a complete solution to a very narrow problem**, and solving
  it should **create the next problem your offer solves** `OpeN4O5myIg`
  `Mst4hreQYl0`. Both halves are load-bearing: *complete* so they get the win,
  *narrow* so you can deliver it fast and free.
- **Carve it out of the product** rather than inventing one. The diagnostic when
  the paid outcome takes a long time: *"Is there a smaller mini version of this
  big outcome that we can just kind of give them a taste?"* `OpeN4O5myIg`
- **Three types**: reveal a problem (an assessment — website speed test, posture
  analysis, termite inspection), a free trial (the Costco sample), or one step of
  a multi-step process (one laser session of six, one coat of paint)
  `Mst4hreQYl0`. **Four delivery vehicles**: software, information, services,
  physical `Mst4hreQYl0`.
- **Naming determines engagement more than anything else.** Four formulas:
  number + outcome + time frame; how to get the good thing without the bad thing;
  adjective + type + good thing; X mistakes that prevent the good thing
  `Mst4hreQYl0`.
- **Do not auto-deliver it — make them reply "yes."** A reply is the strongest
  engagement signal a mail server sees. The welcome email does four things in
  order: create anticipation about an announcement they will hear first; sell them
  on staying subscribed *beyond* the magnet; list the contents by page number to
  make it concrete; *then* ask them to reply. The sequencing is deliberate —
  motivation and attention are at maximum until you hand over the promised thing,
  so spend that window selling the relationship `pLhQOYMGa88`. **Contrarian.**
- **Peel platform-specific bonuses out of owned material.** Chapters that do not
  fit a book get finished to book quality anyway and held as opt-in bonuses, so
  there is a reason to subscribe *there* `pLhQOYMGa88`.
- **Segment on qualifiers captured at opt-in.** He cites a HubSpot study at a
  **791% increase in email ROI** from segmentation, and segments on revenue and
  whether the person has a business `pLhQOYMGa88`. *"It's way less about how good
  the email is and so much more about who's getting it."* Caveat he adds himself:
  people lie upward on the form, and then get content that does not apply.

### Deliverability — the tactics are not optional

The reason for all of it: **16.99% of B2B marketing emails never reach the inbox
at all**, let alone the promotions tab `pLhQOYMGa88`. The cause is domain
reputation, a running score of how good the email you send is, and the remedy is
*a hundred golden BBs rather than one silver bullet*.

The governing principle is not to hunt hacks:

> "Don't try and figure out hacks. Try and simply align what you do with the
> objective of the platform." `pLhQOYMGa88`

The provider wants the highest-quality inbox with the fewest important emails
filtered, because a user who misses an important email switches providers — so
genuinely wanted mail gets delivered.

- **Make it look like a normal email.** Mostly text, a handful of sentences, **no
  images**, one or two links maximum. The opposite — *"it looks like the Vegas
  Strip inside of the email"* — reads as 100% promotion to a filter smart enough
  to classify it. His company sends no images. He also observed that **emails with
  more money language land in the promotions tab at a higher rate**, so he strips
  money language. Landing in the promo tab he calls **email death**
  `pLhQOYMGa88`. **Contrarian.**
- **Make unsubscribing easy.** A captive uninterested subscriber damages
  deliverability more than an unsubscribe does: mailing an uninterested segment
  lowers domain deliverability, which means the people who *do* want your email
  stop getting it. *"If you make it hard they might not unsubscribe, but then they
  go dormant, which is actually worse for you"* `pLhQOYMGa88`. **Contrarian.**
- **Google-native links.** Link a YouTube video rather than another player; put a
  sales letter or VSL in Google Drive. He notes these duct-taped-looking assets
  sometimes convert better than polished ones `pLhQOYMGa88`. **Contrarian**, and
  he offers it as advanced rather than essential.
- **Check it on desktop and on mobile every time.** 58-60% of global internet
  traffic is mobile, and the risk concentrates in image-heavy email, where broken
  images look unprofessional and collapse click-through `pLhQOYMGa88`.

### The parts that get tested

**Split test six things on every campaign** `OpeN4O5myIg`: the subject line, the
opening of the email, the CTAs and links, the landing page, the offer, and the
name of the challenge or lead magnet. *"Those little tests that you can run in
the meantime can sometimes double or triple the throughput of the campaign."*

And the mechanic: **A/B the subject on a small slice, then send the winner to
everyone else** `pLhQOYMGa88`. Note what he splits and what he does not — the
newsletter name stays at the front of every subject, and he splits only what
comes after it:

> "I'm willing to sacrifice a little bit of short-term open rate so that long term
> the audience will just always open it." `pLhQOYMGa88`

Adjacent evidence that page-level detail is worth the test even at scale:
changing only the **banner above an affiliate opt-in form raised signups 60%**,
and **duplicating a tracking link across the thank-you page and an email moved
the number of affiliates who actually promoted by ~25%** `60_7PU9JDIw`.

### Small parts with outsized returns

- **The PS, always.** *"Beyond the headline it's the most read part of the email,
  so not having a PS statement is PS stupid"* `pLhQOYMGa88`. It is the **second
  most read part of any advertising** after the headline, and the PPS is third
  `JDR-R--4HhM`. Because his is usually fun, the times he makes it a CTA it gets
  clicked — the audience has been trained that the PS is worth reading. It is also
  how a deliberately low-pressure newsletter can carry an ask at all: a PS riding
  on a **gift** rather than a pitch `B-ogfFiQpXg`.
- **Preview text.** The line after the bold subject. Left alone, the client takes
  the first 150 characters of the email — often a greeting, worthless. Pull the
  most valuable nugget forward, especially something that induces curiosity. Ten
  seconds of work `pLhQOYMGa88`.
- **Train the audience to click.** Never put the whole blog in the email — put an
  interesting nugget in and link out. He explicitly rejects the minimise-clicks
  advice: the click is a **trained behaviour**, and if the destination is good the
  next click gets more likely `pLhQOYMGa88`. **Contrarian.** Only works when the
  destination reliably pays off.
- **One or two CTAs, one per objective.** He rejects the one-link rule: a
  continued-interest CTA and a buy-now CTA, never more than one per audience or
  objective. Five gets aggressive. The test is whether a given reader has a clear
  reason to click one rather than the other `pLhQOYMGa88`. **Contrarian.**
- **Contextual, never copy-pasted.** Match the CTA to the topic of the content it
  sits in — an email-marketing piece ends with the newsletter, a beginner topic
  points to the community, an enterprise topic points to the workshops
  `pLhQOYMGa88`.
- **Third grade reading level** applies here as everywhere. *"Fancy equals
  friction, simple equals sales"* `JDR-R--4HhM`.

### Reactivation — the highest-return send on a cold list

**The "I owe you" email** `OQf2Ba-Lp_4`. Instead of *we're coming back at you*,
the frame is *we messed up, can we make it up to you*:

- **Subject and opening line:** *I owe you a free checkup*, or *I owe you $175* —
  naming the actual dollar value.
- **Body:** it turns out that when you signed up we did not communicate a
  component of our offer that we gave other customers, and we want to do right by
  you.
- **Bold and underline "at no cost to you"**, because people scan.
- **Split test it** by sending one version one week and the alternative the next,
  comparing open and response rates.

He sized reactivation at a **20-30% revenue lift, all of it profit, compounding
as the list grows** `OQf2Ba-Lp_4`.

The same instinct at onboarding speed: at Gym Launch, the way they compressed
time-to-value for a new client was to **email the client's own list to get them a
sale in the first seven days** — the fastest win available to anyone is the
audience they already own and have not monetised `BYpTRiRqS1Y`.

And the strategic reason all of this works at all: **nurture sequences are
compressed brand** `KhFlD54nQrY`. A well-researched free PDF is an exchange in
miniature, and the person extrapolates from it what buying from you would be
like. Five-day challenges do the same at scale — *"you're microwaving the
prospect rather than slow cooking it, which is kind of the normal branding"*
`OpeN4O5myIg`. The implication runs both ways: **a business with real brand needs
challenges less, and a business with none needs them more.**

## Where the corpus disagrees with itself

| Position | Where |
|---|---|
| You need a regular cadence for **deliverability and domain reputation** | `OpeN4O5myIg` |

**4. Repetition.** *"I'll say it in different ways, but the same content over and

## One number to check before it leaves the building

Four flags in the email material specifically:

- **The campaign funnel does not close.** 83,000 opens × ~5% clicked ≈ **4,200
  clicks**, but he reports **10,000 registrations** `OpeN4O5myIg`. Registrations
  cannot exceed clicks. Either the click rate is on the send rather than on
  openers, or the 5% is mis-stated. **Do not reproduce the funnel as a chain.**
  The endpoints (464,000 sent → 10,000 registered → 800 buyers) are internally
  consistent and are the safe figures to use.
- **$6 per email is rounded up.** $2.4M ÷ 464,000 = **$5.17**. He says *"roughly
  six bucks"* `OpeN4O5myIg` — about a 16% overstatement on the number he tells
  you to plan the year around. Plan on five.
- **"$2.4 million in additional profit"** `OpeN4O5myIg` is the quote; the
  arithmetic is **gross sales** (800 × $2,000, plus the ascension). His argument
  is that CAC was $0 so the gross profit drops to the bottom line — but the cost
  of *delivering* the $2,000 product is never netted. Call it revenue.
- **The preview-text figure carries two readings in one item.** It is stated both
  as *~24% of people read the preview before opening* and as *a 24% increase*
  `pLhQOYMGa88`. Those are different quantities. Use it as a reason to write the
  line, not as a number in a deck.

The README's standing rule applies to the rest: a single pass of speech
recognition. **35-45:1 ROI, 791% from segmentation and 391% from 60-second
speed-to-lead are all cited from other people's studies** — check them against
the original before any of them leaves the building.

## When it applies

- **You already own a list, or another channel is building one.** The 35-45:1
  figure presumes the contact was acquired and paid for somewhere else
  `pLhQOYMGa88`. Email does not replace acquisition; it monetises it.
- **Your audience is demographically wider than one social platform.** Email
  consumption is roughly uniform across every age bracket, which is his argument
  for mastering it over any single social channel `pLhQOYMGa88`.
- **You have an unworked list of non-buyers.** That is the quarterly cleanup's
  precondition and the whole basis of the $2.4M case `OpeN4O5myIg`.
- **Waiting is the expensive mistake.** He calls delaying email *one of the
  biggest mistakes of his career* — he did not email at Gym Launch until two or
  three years in, and adding email follow-up to Skool produced a **40% conversion
  lift** `pLhQOYMGa88`.

It applies **less** where you have no list and no other channel feeding one — in
that case the work is the lead magnet and the opt-in, not the send.
