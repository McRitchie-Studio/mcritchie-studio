# Offer construction — what you sell and on what terms

**Contrarian:** in parts, marked below · **Fit:** all three

## The claim

An offer is not a price with a description attached. It is four levers of
perceived value, a believability structure, and a price that is tested rather
than chosen — and the corpus says most operators get all three wrong in the
same direction, by being vague about the outcome and cheap about the number.

## The mechanism

### The value equation — four levers, two you already know about

Value has four elements (`reisEL_D7xc`): **dream outcome**, **perceived
likelihood of achievement**, **time delay**, and **effort and sacrifice**. The
dream outcome sets the price ceiling. The other three are what separate two
products that promise the same result — his example is a $20 weight-loss PDF
and liposuction, both of which promise you get lean.

The working form is three questions: *how risk-free, how easy, how fast* — plus
*do they actually want it*. Perceived likelihood is why the surgeon with 10,000
operations prices above the one on day one. Time delay inverted is speed. Effort
inverted is ease.

Selling to wealthy buyers runs the same equation with the weights changed
(`KhFlD54nQrY`): the richer the buyer, the more they want the outcome
*guaranteed* — and he is precise that you need the **perception** of a
guarantee, which is what reputation buys, not necessarily the guarantee itself.
The move is to do what someone already does for them in half the time at double
the price. Never sell a wealthy buyer on money saved; sell on time saved.

### Proof, promise, plan — the believability structure

Three videos carry the same three-part opener, derived by him rewatching his own
top 35 educational videos (`Jmkq5RLjm0U`, `HVOubeXUcx0`, `Z2tgZC_XkT4`):

| Part | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Proof** | Why they should believe you at all — usually numbers you produced |
| **Promise** | What they get or learn, stated exactly |
| **Plan** | What happens next and how the promise gets fulfilled |

Order is flexible — lead with whichever is strongest. For a business audience he
leads with **proof**, because the unspoken question above every cold prospect is
*why should I listen to you*, asked before their attention is fully on. The
contrast that makes it usable: **entertainment** intros confirm the thumbnail;
**education** intros prove the teacher.

The partnership variant adds a fourth beat — promise, proof, plan, *then the
offer* — plus a this-is-you-now versus this-is-you-with-me graph with real
arithmetic, and the instruction to make **your** side of the work look heavier
than theirs (`Z2tgZC_XkT4`).

### Damaging admissions — say the bad part first

**Contrarian.** For an offer whose obstacle is disbelief rather than desire,
open with the limitations before any benefit (`MH-IMJxbUY4`). Two effects, and
the second is the one operators miss: admitting the offer is too good to be true
makes it **less** good and therefore **more** believable, and prospects
**self-qualify** — the ones who can live with the trade say so themselves.

Two sibling items sharpen it:

- **Advertise the negatives** (`jiCGLDhUCHY`, contrarian, credited to Rory
  Sutherland). State the drawbacks and find the segment that reads them as
  neutral. The arbitrage is the gap between what one segment tolerates and what
  the market penalizes.
- **Specific on negatives, vague on positives** (`Mst4hreQYl0`, contrarian).
  Name their problems with total precision and keep your promises deliberately
  general. A prospect who believes you diagnosed them correctly assumes you can
  fix them — and precision on the negative *buys you the room* to be vaguer on
  the positive, which markets more compliantly.

### Price the result, not the hour

Hourly pricing is commoditization by construction (`3yAiVjcImQ4`): nobody knows
what an hour should cost except by comparing it to other people's hours. Price
against the dream outcome instead. His worked example: a music lawyer prices
against *you want your music sold to a record label*, not against six hours of
anyone's time.

How the price is **structured** also tells the prospect your incentives. Flat
fee plus a performance commission makes them believe the result will happen,
because you only win if they do. He notes 100% commission reads as an amazing
offer and is why many use it — but it exposes you to clients who may not
succeed. Flat-plus-performance is the dialled-back version.

### AB offers and anchors

**Always present two versions** (`3yAiVjcImQ4`). A choice between two things
converts differently than a yes/no on one — chocolate or vanilla, Monday or
Tuesday. The B option must be a genuine offer you would fulfil, not a strawman.

Two shapes in the corpus:

- **Anchor on both sides** (`3yAiVjcImQ4`) — a high anchor above the core and a
  deliberately *close* downsell below it, so the arithmetic the prospect runs
  favours the middle. The attorney case: $25,000 no-commission anchor above,
  $2,000-$3,000 DIY below, $5,800-plus-commission in the middle. He is explicit
  he is *not* generally a fan of downsells and used one only because the
  business had many unqualified buyers.
- **A prepaid annual anchor beside a quarterly core** (`Wr6n_zNKvMk`) — the
  billable is identical; all you are debating is who gets the free item, and
  they get it by committing for the year.

### Guarantees

One concrete guarantee play, for trades and local B2B (`0EqJD2o-Mnk`): make the
service faster, more reliable or easier, guarantee **around that**, and raise
price 20-40%. The guarantee is *if we do not meet any of these qualifications, I
give you all my profit back* — so a miss puts you back at exactly today's price
and a hit keeps all the juice. The scripted competitive argument: if nobody else
offers this, it is because they are not confident they will be on time and on
budget.

### When to raise price

Four independent signals in the corpus, all pointing up:

| Signal | Source | The move |
|---|---|---|
| Close rate at or above 80% | `OQf2Ba-Lp_4` | There is room in the price |
| "Exceptionally good" close rate | `3yAiVjcImQ4` | You are underpriced, not good at selling |
| First 10-20 case studies are done | `reisEL_D7xc` | Step up 20% every five sales |
| Below ~$3-5M revenue, unoptimized | `OQf2Ba-Lp_4` | Biggest single lever available |

**The stepping rule** (`reisEL_D7xc`): do the first 10-20 free or cheap for case
studies, then raise 20% every five sales, tracking total money per fixed number
of sales calls. Stop when conversion rate × price starts falling. That point is
the most units at the highest price.

**The breakeven rule** (`OQf2Ba-Lp_4`): the test is not whether close rate
falls, it is whether it falls *below breakeven*. On his worked case a 10% raise
from 82% close rate breaks even at 65%, and the raise drops straight to the
bottom line — he sizes it at roughly a 25% increase in net profit.

**The mechanism behind it** (`OQf2Ba-Lp_4`, contrarian): raising price raises the
customer's **conviction that you can deliver**, which is perceived likelihood in
the value equation — higher price, higher emotional investment, better results,
less demanding customers, more money to actually deliver. He fences it: this is
normal goods and services, *not* luxury positioning, which works differently.
The failure mode is pricing so low the buyer reads you as a duct-tape operation.

### Trim the offer before you price it

**Trim and stack** (`N5MExtki_VI`): list every asset and deliverable you have,
then cut and recombine into the leanest version people actually want. Cut on
what clients **say** they value, not on what is cheapest to keep — he deleted
Slack access and weekly group calls because surveys named them least favourite,
and promoted the annual in-person event from bonus to the core reason to buy.

## When it applies

The value equation and proof/promise/plan are general. The rest carries
conditions worth honouring:

- **Damaging admissions** are for offers blocked by **disbelief**, not by
  desire. If nobody wants it, admitting flaws does nothing.
- **The negatives play** needs real, visible drawbacks a defined segment is
  indifferent to. Not for a commodity where every buyer has the same tolerance.
- **Price-the-result** needs the result to be nameable in the buyer's language.
- **Close-rate-as-underpricing** needs enough sales conversations for the rate
  to mean anything, and he pairs it with a traffic increase first, so fewer
  closes at a higher price still nets more.
- **Free first** is for a brand-new offer with no proof, and he says ask for the
  four returns explicitly up front — conversion, referral, testimonial, feedback
  (`reisEL_D7xc`).

## Where the corpus disagrees with itself

**Do you act on a price complaint?** Two answers, and the boundary is *who
complained*.

- `0EqJD2o-Mnk` — price complaints are noise: *"Customers will always want it
  cheaper, they will always want it faster, they always want a guarantee. These
  will not change."* He is talking about customers who have bought for six
  years.
- `-j8_YCWZ05Q` — a complaint from your **ideal** customer is a segmentation
  signal: add a lower tier. From a **non-ideal** person, ignore it — the price
  is filtering correctly. His own product went from $99 to a tier 11 times
  cheaper at $9, because hobbyists genuinely were the ideal early user.

Rex's reading: both hold, and the question to ask first is *is the complainer
the person we want*.

**Does cutting price do anything?** `BYpTRiRqS1Y` measured a 20% cut and got
**zero** churn change — he had stayed inside the same psychological band, so all
he did was cut topline. `-j8_YCWZ05Q` built an 11x cheaper tier that worked. The
condition boundary neither states outright: a cut inside a band buys nothing; an
order-of-magnitude cut creates a different product for a different segment.

## One number that does not reconcile

The AB-offer case in `Wr6n_zNKvMk` states an 80/20 split between a $189
quarterly and a $360 annual, and reports the blend as **"roughly $270 per
door"** against a $208 target, with the anchor adding **"about $80 per door."**
Run it: 0.8 × $189 + 0.2 × $360 = **$223**, and the anchor's contribution is
0.2 × ($360 − $189) = **$34**. Either the split or the blend is mis-heard. The
*shape* of the play survives — a prepaid anchor lifts the blended average above
target — but do not carry $270 or $80 into a client model.

## In his words

> "how risk-free can I make it? How easy can I make it? How fast can I make it?"

> "when you even offer this too good to be true, you make it less good. So that
> it's more believable."

> "No one has a really good idea of what an hourly rate should be unless they
> just look at other people's hourly rates"

> "you just keep going up by 20% until the conversion rate times the price goes
> down"

> "If we don't meet any of these qualifications, I'll give you all my profit
> back."

## Rex's read for our three clients

- **industries** — a welding shop is the exact case `0EqJD2o-Mnk` describes:
  quote the result and the standard, not the hour, then attach the on-time,
  on-budget guarantee nobody else in the trade offers and take the 20-40%. If
  the shop is closing most of what it quotes, it is underpriced, and the raise
  funds the hire that is the real constraint.
- **turf-monster** — damaging admissions is the play for a pick'em product
  people suspect is too good: name the limits in the ad copy and let the
  skeptics self-qualify. AB the entry rather than asking yes/no on one price.
- **operator-brand** — proof/promise/plan is the whole opener for a studio with
  a story and no track record; the proof we have is labour, not outcomes, which
  is its own item on [`audience-and-avatar.md`](audience-and-avatar.md). Price
  the shipped result, not the agent-hour.
