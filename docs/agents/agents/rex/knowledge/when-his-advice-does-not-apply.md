# When his advice does not apply

**Kind:** synthesis · **Sources:** 35 videos · **Contrarian:** by construction ·
**Fit:** all three

Every other card in this directory says what to do. This one says where the
method stops.

An agent built on one practitioner's method is dangerous exactly where that
method has edges, because the method does not announce them — the confident
sentence and the out-of-scope sentence sound identical. The corpus does record
the edges, but scattered: mostly in the `condition` field of individual items,
where a limit sits attached to the one play it bounds and is invisible to anyone
reading the doctrine. This card collects them.

It is the sibling of [`where-he-contradicts-himself`](where-he-contradicts-himself.md).
That card maps where he believes two things. This one maps where he says **"not
here"** — and, in several places, where his own first-person evidence
contradicts his general rule.

---

## First, the trap: his default answer to "that doesn't apply to me" is no

Read this before using anything below, because the most common use of a limits
card is to excuse a client from work they do not want to do, and he has already
ruled on that move.

| Where | What he does with the exemption claim |
|---|---|
| `reisEL_D7xc` | Lists the channels he has heard written off — Facebook, Google, TikTok, direct mail, radio, content, outreach — and rewrites the sentence mechanically: *X doesn't work for us* becomes *I don't know how to make X work for us*, which is a solvable problem |
| `reisEL_D7xc` | On demonstrating rather than describing the result, he explicitly forbids the excuse that it does not apply to your business |
| `XsWSvz-aewA` | On engineering proof into the service motion, his answer to the operator who says it does not work for their business is flat — then you are not going to make it |
| `qpQvdBFW_yI` | Introducing the referral plays, he insists they are not confined to brick-and-mortar or service businesses. The only permitted framing is *how can I make this work for my business* |

> "All advertising works. It's just a matter of efficiency."

> "I promise you that is The Voice of the loser"

So a limit earns a place on this card only if **he drew it himself**, or if
**his own evidence cuts against his own rule.** Everything below meets that bar.
Nothing below is a licence to skip the work.

**One thing has changed about the trap, and Rex must hold both halves.** The
fourth row above is about the referral plays specifically — and in
`uYds0zcAFWM` he takes the opposite side on exactly that subject, naming two
conditions under which referrals will never appear and saying so about his own
prior belief (§3). So *"it won't work for my business"* is still the voice of
the loser as a **reflex**, and is still the wrong first move. It is no longer
automatically wrong as a **conclusion**, on this one subject, once the two
conditions have actually been tested. The discipline: run the test, then
conclude. Never conclude first.

## 1. Gate zero — none of it applies to a supply-constrained business

**Sources:** `OQf2Ba-Lp_4`, `j2TZMFkj71Q`, `BYpTRiRqS1Y`, `N5MExtki_VI`,
`Mst4hreQYl0` · five videos, his most repeated limit

This is the largest boundary in the corpus and the easiest to skip, because it
is a question rather than a doctrine. He asks it *before* analysis, and the
answer decides whether marketing is the right work at all.

> "If we double the lead flow of the business, can you handle that?"

> "Can you handle 20 people a week?"

The second phrasing is deliberate. On `BYpTRiRqS1Y` he starts to ask *could you
sell* 20 a week and corrects himself mid-sentence to *can you handle* 20 a week,
because the two answers differ and only the second one is about capacity.

**If the answer is no, every demand-side card in this directory is out of
scope.** The job is the four supply moves (`N5MExtki_VI`): raise the price,
change the delivery ratio, productize delivery, or hire people to deliver — and
he notes each one commits you to a different kind of company, so the choice is
which problem you would rather own.

The trap inside the trap: on `OQf2Ba-Lp_4` he sequences conversion fixes *ahead*
of budget increases for a specific reason — fixing the landing page and the
creative may double or triple lead flow at the current spend, at which point the
owner discovers he does not have enough technicians. **Conversion work can buy
you the supply problem at a discount.** So the capacity question gates funnel
work too, not only spend.

## 2. The low-ticket exception — the back end cannot always be engineered

**Sources:** `-UvmyixFszw` (the rule), `xZ8d9g6BcKM` (the exception),
corroborated by `qsXxckCbci0` and `0S5xsICW8qg`

**This is one of the two most load-bearing limits in the corpus** (the other is
§3), because the rule it bounds is one of his hardest-stated positions and the
exception is buried in a video about frozen yogurt.

The rule, from `-UvmyixFszw`:

> "everyone thinks they have a marketing problem but typically it's because they
> have a monetization issue"

His mechanism is that growth is unlocked by raising cash per customer, because
channels cost different amounts and a high enough cash-per-show makes *all* of
them affordable at once — referral partners, cold traffic, affiliates, direct
mail, social. It comes from the back end, not the front.

The exception, from `xZ8d9g6BcKM`, where he is pricing a frozen yogurt store:

> "it's very difficult to acquire customers with paid advertising profitably"

At a yogurt-cup ticket, with margin only on that cup, he says the only profitable
acquisition is **word of mouth or affiliates** — other businesses that send you
business — and he notes the alternative window has closed: years ago Google
search was cheap enough to drive pay-per-click to store locations, and now it is
not. He also observes that this is why a lot of these businesses raise funding.

**Read the two together and the general rule acquires a precondition it does not
state on its own: there has to be a back end you can reach.** Some businesses
cannot engineer a payable cash-per-show at all. For them "it's a monetization
problem" is not a diagnosis, it is a dead end, and the remaining lanes are
referral and affiliate.

Two other items corroborate from different angles:

- `qsXxckCbci0` — low-priced products need a loop where one acquired customer
  brings at least 1.1 more, or paid acquisition eventually fails at scale.
- `0S5xsICW8qg` — his depth-beats-breadth argument is scoped: it applies when
  the purchase requires trust or carries risk, and is **less relevant for cheap
  impulse products where breadth is the whole game.** So at a low ticket, the
  niche-intensity doctrine weakens too, not just the paid doctrine.

**Note the collision with the trap above.** *All advertising works, it's just a
matter of efficiency* and *paid advertising cannot acquire these customers
profitably* are both his. The reconciliation is inside the hedge — efficiency
has a floor set by your margin — but an agent quoting the first line at a
low-ticket client will burn the budget before the hedge arrives. **Quote the
exception.**

**And read §3 immediately after this one.** The escape hatch this section hands
a low-ticket business is *word of mouth or affiliates* — and §3 is where he says
word of mouth is not available to everyone. For a business that fails both
tests, the two exits named here reduce to one.

## 3. Word of mouth is conditional — and he names his own prior belief as broken

**Source:** `uYds0zcAFWM` · his most explicit self-reversal in the corpus

**This is the single largest addition to this card**, because referral and word
of mouth are the fallback that three other sections and half this directory
route traffic to. He now says the fallback is not always there.

> "my belief set is broken because I was very hardcore on like everything can
> grow on word of mouth period"

He names **two independent killers**, and either one weakens virality while both
together end it:

| Killer | Mechanism | Where it bites |
|---|---|---|
| **Disincentive to share** | If you serve two similar businesses, a customer has a literal reason not to tell anyone what is helping them — they do not want a competitor to know | B2B, explicitly. He scopes this one to B2B by name |
| **Low communication density** | There has to be frequent communication between your customers and other potential customers. Without the occasion, there is no referral even with goodwill | Any market where buyers do not talk to each other |

**His worked example is a metal shop specialising in aerotech**, and Rex must
read the roles correctly before reaching for the analogy: in his telling the
metal shop is the **customer** — the business being advised is the software
vendor selling *into* aerotech shops. The shop is the one who will not refer.

> "And if he is talking to competitors, like why would he want to share all my
> stuff?"

Both conditions fail at once there: no density of communication (why would he
talk to rival aerotech firms) and an active disincentive (why would he hand them
his edge). His conclusion is not *try harder at referrals* — it is that you do
not have to worry about virality at all, and something else is the gold
standard:

> "The gold standard should absolutely be revenue retention bar none. Full stop."

He states this **immediately after** the aerotech example, in the same breath,
and scopes it as wide as he scopes anything: no matter what business you are in,
B2B or B2C, you want recurring or reoccurring revenue. **So the prescription for
a business with dead virality is revenue retention, not better referral asks.**
The measurement he gives is exact — of the customers you had on day one, what
percentage are still buying twelve months later — and he distinguishes it from
logo retention on purpose.

**Which side of the line a business sits on:**

- **Consumer sits on the live side.** He says so directly — the virality is
  typically more on the consumer side, nobody hides a good soda or a shirt that
  fits, and those customers talk to similar people often.
- **Specialist B2B sits on the dead side**, when buyers compete with each other
  and rarely meet.

**The collision this creates inside the corpus, both sides verified.** In
`qpQvdBFW_yI` he presents referrals as universally available:

> "30% of your business coming from referrals no matter you're at 100 million or
> 100,000 a year still grows your business by 30%"

That is offered as scale-invariant and unconditioned. `uYds0zcAFWM` conditions
it. **The reconciliation is chronological — the 14-years video is him revising
the earlier position, and he says so out loud.** Rex uses the conditioned
version and cites both.

**The partial precedent he already had.** On `KhFlD54nQrY` he says the smaller
and more connected the niche, the more word of mouth matters. That is the
density variable, stated from the optimistic side. What `uYds0zcAFWM` adds is
that **small is not the same as dense** — a niche can be tiny *and*
disconnected, which is precisely the aerotech case. **Flagged as inference:** he
does not reconcile the two statements himself.

**One more boundary on the same subject, from `neTSqOAMgao`:** word of mouth is
the only one of his six acquisition channels that compounds —

> "with referrals word of mouth it is quadratic which means it's exponential and
> so it is the hardest one to achieve"

— and the compounding starts **only when the product is remarkable**, literally
remark-worthy. A merely good product produces linear referrals. So even on the
live side of the line, referral volume is a verdict on the product, not a
campaign you can run.

## 4. The scale trigger that may never fire

**Sources:** `KhFlD54nQrY`, `VBoRLJimVzc`, `w7g08dVTwaE` vs `uYds0zcAFWM` ·
**Rex's synthesis — flagged as inference**

This is not a limit he states. It is a structural consequence of §3 that nobody
in the corpus joins up, and it deadlocks three separate plays.

**Three plays, in three videos, all gate on the same event: an unprompted
referral.**

| Play | The gate it names | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Advertise only enough to keep iterating, then pour gasoline | Scale when people cannot help telling everybody they talked to that day | `KhFlD54nQrY` |
| After the first sales, fix the product before you market harder | The gate to raising spend is a customer who refers unprompted | `VBoRLJimVzc` |
| When do you start charging? | Start charging the moment free clients start referring you | `w7g08dVTwaE` |

> "start charging once people start referring that's when you know you were good
> enough to start charging"

**For a business whose virality is genuinely dead, none of these three triggers
can ever fire.** The operator following them waits forever: never scaling spend,
never leaving free pricing, never done fixing the product. The advice does not
fail loudly — it fails by never issuing a verdict.

The corpus half-notices this once: the `w7g08dVTwaE` item itself carries a
condition pointing at the virality boundary. Nobody carries the observation to
the other two.

**Rex's substitute, and he must label it as his own.** Where §3's two tests say
virality is dead, the three gates need a replacement signal — and the video that
kills the trigger names the replacement in the same passage: **revenue
retention.** Use a retention threshold as the readiness gate instead of a
referral event. That the substitution is legitimate is **Rex's inference**; that
retention is the gold standard for such a business is **his statement**.

## 5. The 3:1 LTV:CAC benchmark is a software number

**Source:** `uYds0zcAFWM`

The ratio everyone quotes has a precondition he states explicitly, and for most
real businesses the correct number is not close to 3:1.

> "the 3:1 only matters at the companies that have 100% scale, 100% leverage
> across all three components"

He splits a business into three functions — **attraction** (advertising),
**conversion** (sales), **delivery** (fulfilment) — and says the ratio you need
is set by how manual each one is. It is scored across all three, not chosen from
a table.

| Shape | What it looks like | Ratio he gives |
|---|---|---|
| **Fully automated** | Paid ads → checkout page → software delivery | 3:1 works long-haul; **1.5:1 can survive** at scale |
| **Fully manual** | Manual outbound → one-to-one sales → concierge delivery | Wants **20:1 or more**, shoots for 30+, **15:1 is the floor** |

He reports having made the vast majority of his material wealth at **30:1 or
higher**, multiple times. And he notes the manual case is the common one, not
the exception: 78% of American businesses are service-based, while the 3:1
literature was written for software with near-100% gross margins.

**Two consequences that change advice, not just arithmetic:**

- **At 5:1 or 8:1 in a manual business, his prescription is do not scale.**

  > "instead of trying to scale something that's at you know 5 one or 8 one I'm
  > just going to continue to tinker"

  Stay at one location, keep working the model and the offer until the economics
  are wide enough to scale into. He concedes this takes longer than people
  expect and that the target ratios sound unrealistic to most people.

- **The ratio has to absorb the lumpiness of hiring.** New SDRs are unproductive
  or never work out; new salespeople lose opportunities while being paid to
  close fewer; recruiting a senior person costs 25-30% of first-year pay before
  you learn whether they are any good. At 3:1 that wipes out instantly. And as
  you push into colder markets a smaller percentage converts, so acquisition
  cost rises at the same moment management layers are added.

**The related correction:** use **LTGP** — lifetime *gross profit* — not LTV.

> "that's only true if you sell information media or software, something has
> zero incremental cost"

A customer paying $100 a month for ten months is worth $1,000 only at zero
incremental cost. At $20 of fulfilment per $100, the real figure is $800. For
software the two numbers converge, which is exactly why the literature never
drew the distinction.

**Rex's rule: never quote 3:1 without first scoring the three functions.** For a
manual service business it is off by a factor of five or more, and quoting it
authorises a spend level that will not survive the first three hires.

## 6. One product, one avatar, one channel has a stated expiry

**Sources:** `neTSqOAMgao` (the $3M boundary), `VBoRLJimVzc` (a $1-2M version of
the same rule)

The focus doctrine is one of his most repeated, and he bounds it by revenue
himself.

> "the advice pretty much holds which is one product one avatar on one channel"

On `neTSqOAMgao` he scopes it to roughly **$3 million a year in revenue**. The
constraint it manages is attention, not time — too many pans on the burner and
the heat is not concentrated enough while a competitor puts every ounce into one
problem.

**What replaces it above the line is a different kind of work entirely.** For
$3M → $10M:

> "the lifetime gross profit per customer has to be higher"

Not more channels, not more products for their own sake — improve the product,
reduce churn if recurring, optimise pricing, or add one high-value service level
or back end. He pre-empts the operational-complexity objection and answers it
flatly: yes it adds complexity, and people who make $10 million are more skilled
than people who make $3 million, so that skill is the one to acquire.

**He scopes even that.** The $3M→$10M prescription is aimed at high-ticket
coaches in a niche, and he adds that there are absolutely exceptions.

**The number is not stable across videos, and Rex should say so rather than
pick one.** On `VBoRLJimVzc` the same rule is a sub-$1-2M rule — *"one Avatar
one product one channel this is the simplest formula to getting $2 million
plus"* — with a different expiry attached: channel diversification starts
mattering when an **acquirer** is assessing risk, so it also expires when you
sell the business. Two videos, two thresholds, same doctrine. **Treat the band
as $1M-$3M rather than quoting a precise number he does not hold precisely.**

**A related boundary on niching, same video.** Before switching niches he asks:

> "have you reached the market cap the answer is no of course not"

The reason to niche down is to avoid fighting bigger competitors, so it stops
applying once you can beat them — he says there IS a time to become Tony
Robbins, but most people are not there. His diagnosis for the agency coach who
wanted to broaden: you have risen to your level of incompetence, so switching
means rising to the same level somewhere else, while halving the first business.

## 7. "Replicate before you iterate" is scoped to skills only

**Source:** `zNJ5JzEJgyo`

He states the catchphrase and then immediately draws its boundary, which is the
part that does not travel with the catchphrase.

> "You duplicate the skill so you know you can do it. That's different than
> replicating a story and replicating an identity"

**Copy:** capabilities and mechanics. To learn to sell, sit next to the person
making calls; to learn door-knocking, walk with the best door-knocker on the
team. Duplicate the motion until you have proved to yourself you can do it, then
iterate.

**Do not copy:** story, identity, positioning, narrative, voice, visual identity.
His structural reason is not taste:

> "You will never win cuz you aren't going to beat them at being them"

He frames identity-level copying as a status transaction — if you copy them you
have accepted second place and declared them inherently first. If others copy
*you*, that is a good sign.

## 8. His own swipe-file evidence: the copied page converted worse

**Source:** `zNJ5JzEJgyo` · first-person, measured, against the general rule

Running a local gym, a competitor moved into his market and was outspending him.
He copied their landing page wholesale — same page, logo swapped, roughly 90%
identical.

> "His page did worse than mine did. Why am I copying this guy? I actually just
> got worse outcomes from this"

The conclusion he drew was not *test harder* but a question about the premise.
**A proven asset does not carry its performance across to a different business**,
because the competitor's winning page is winning inside their traffic, their
offer and their story — and none of that came with the copy.

This is the concrete counter-case to swipe-file marketing, and it is the only
place in the corpus where he reports copying a competitor's asset and measuring
the result.

## 9. Competitor research: off in one video, market evidence in another

**Sources:** `zNJ5JzEJgyo` vs `1IhvZC-Wpqs` · a live disagreement, both sides
flagged in the corpus itself

| Position | Where |
|---|---|
| Turn the competitor feed off. He turned off all the ads in his newsfeed and stopped subscribing to competitor lists, because consuming their material primes you and makes your output derivative. The corollary he draws is that originality is cheap — it is easy if you are simply not looking at everyone else's material | `zNJ5JzEJgyo` |
| Read competitor offers as market data. To the operator shaking his fist at competitors selling gimmicky short-duration offers: stop saying *this is the truth* and start saying *this is their truth right now* | `1IhvZC-Wpqs` |

> "A lot of people say stay in tune with your competitors, consume everyone
> else's stuff. I actually don't feel that way"

> "they're probably marketing it because it's working for them"

His own story on the first side is the reason it is not merely a preference:
during the Gym Launch years he subscribed to a competitor's list and found his
own emails, 100% word for word, nothing swapped. He was about to copy a
competitor who was already copying him.

**The reconciliation is probably creative intake versus market research — stop
consuming competitors' *creative*, keep reading competitors' *offers*.** Nobody
in the corpus states that boundary. **Flagged as inference.**

Rex's rule until it resolves: read a competitor's offer and price as evidence
about what buyers currently accept; do not read their creative while writing
yours.

## 10. "No story" is not a marketing problem — the precondition on having a brand

**Source:** `zNJ5JzEJgyo`

The prescription is two-part, and the second part is a precondition on every
brand-building play in this directory.

Format is copyable and therefore worthless as a moat — he has watched people
reproduce the look, feel, sound and edits of his content and concluded the
imitation fails at the level of substance, not craft. So: fix the story and the
brand takes care of itself, **provided the story is true.** And if there is no
story:

> "if you don't have the story to tell, then do the [expletive] that's worth
> doing that no one else is willing to do"

He treats the absence of a story as a **diagnosis of the work, not of the
marketing.** The supporting case is an agency owner selling to real-estate
agents who had never been one: the prescription was not a marketing or sales
hack but to pause the agency, take twelve weeks, pass the licensing test and go
sell houses. In the first month the client discovered his own leads were not as
good as he thought, which was why nobody kept paying him.

**The limit on the limit, which he states:** this is not a play for an operator
who already has an earned but untold story. That one just needs telling.

**The B2B form of the same idea, from `neTSqOAMgao`:**

> "in my opinion b2b advertising is all about demonstrating expertise in advance"

He scopes this explicitly to B2B and does not offer it as a rule for consumer
advertising. The mechanism is the problem list: because you know every problem
that will occur, your marketing speaks in specific pains rather than features
and promises — his illustration is a gym owner who knows Susie will always
complain about the music and the bathrooms, a line a marketer who never ran a
gym could not write. **For a B2B client this converts §10's "earn the story"
from a character point into the actual ad strategy.**

## 11. Being right is not a marketing strategy — the limit on authenticity

**Sources:** `1IhvZC-Wpqs` vs `zNJ5JzEJgyo` and `SCi464zfAUM`

Across `zNJ5JzEJgyo` and `SCi464zfAUM` the differentiation play is authenticity:
say the two to four things you actually believe that clash with your category's
uniform, because once an audience can predict you it stops paying attention. He
calls it his favourite way to differentiate the portfolio companies.

Then his own first-person counter-evidence, from `1IhvZC-Wpqs`:

> "my marketing that talked about that because I knew it was true didn't get
> people to respond"

Starting out in fitness he marketed the sustainable, correct answer — count your
macros, do resistance training. He knew it was true. It produced **no customers.**
The fix was not better copy on the same message; it was switching the front end
to short-duration challenges and detoxes, and moving the macros conversation to
after the first result had landed.

**So authenticity differentiates the person; it does not qualify the message.**
The reconciling model he gives is the ham and the garlic: the garlic is the true
solution, the ham is what the prospect will currently accept, and you wrap one in
the other. He is explicit that this is not licence to sell something false — the
ham is a true early offer and the garlic still gets delivered.

**The operational form of this limit: "we will say what we really believe" is a
positioning decision, not an offer. It cannot be the answer to "why is nobody
buying."**

## 12. Content is not a funnel when it attracts the wrong audience

**Sources:** `Jmkq5RLjm0U`, `HVOubeXUcx0`, `dMZ-n2KSlxE` · three videos

> "these people do not become these people. Not in any appreciable rate that we
> would build a business around."

The standard model is wide entertaining content at the top feeding narrow
educational content below. Measured against his own book sales, email opt-ins
and portfolio applications, he reports three **parallel** audiences instead:
entertainment viewers consume more entertainment, education viewers consume more
education, and the first does not become the second. The same shape holds by
topic — relationship viewers want more relationship content.

**The consequence is the limit:** content that attracts the wrong audience is not
a top of funnel, it is a dead end you paid for. And on `dMZ-n2KSlxE`:

> "If you make content and you judge it by views I think that's dumb"

His test case is a video of a grandma slapping him in public: it would get views,
get him no new believers, and be shown to people interested in humour.

**Both limits carry their own scope**, which he states:

- The views objection explicitly does not apply if the business **is**
  entertainment — if you sell anything else, he says you are an educator.
- The funnel objection applies when content exists to source buyers. If the goal
  is reach for its own sake, it does not bite.

**A third scope, from `UGEc9-7X3OQ`, that Rex keeps getting wrong.** His
give-in-content / ask-in-ads split —

> "all of your content is only give and then the ads are the ask"

— **requires a paid budget to carry the asks.** Without ads running there is
nowhere for the ask to go, and the same video's no-CTAs-in-content rule then
removes the only remaining place to put it. **A client with content and no ad
spend cannot run this split**, and the corpus flags the collision itself: in
`qpQvdBFW_yI` the million-customer bring-a-friend campaign puts the ask
*inside* the content, relentlessly, and lifts word of mouth to 25-30%. Both are
his. **For a client with no ad budget, follow the second.**

## 13. Unit-economics advice is void while attribution is broken

**Sources:** `HGZOxBfnF-E`, `OQf2Ba-Lp_4`

The LTGP:CAC apparatus that runs through half this directory has a precondition
he states out loud, on a railing store reporting an LTV:CAC of 0.41:1 while
comfortably profitable:

> "So, we have numbers, but they're not useful."

A ratio that says you should be losing money, on a business that is not losing
money, is **false data, not a false business.** He names the alternative that
would make it true — referral and repeat sales being credited to ads, meaning
money burning out the back — and concludes that either way **there is no basis to
spend more or less until attribution is fixed.**

The same boundary lands on his most quoted spend argument. On `OQf2Ba-Lp_4` he
breaks an owner's emotional ceiling with *if I gave you five dollars for every
one, what would your budget be* — and that question is **only valid once
attribution is trustworthy.** Otherwise the ratio may be an artifact.

His honest option when attribution is broken is to shut the ads off and watch
what happens to sales. He offers it as the ballsier option, accepts softer
evidence when the owner objects, and notes the cost of accepting it: it is still
not tracked, so no optimization is possible.

**`neTSqOAMgao` adds a second way the number lies, in the opposite direction.**
Cold paid traffic is usually not cold — word of mouth touched those buyers
first, and the ad only closed the loop, so paid gets credited with demand it did
not create. He applies the correction to his own most-quoted benchmark: the
36:1 and 100:1 returns on ad spend were produced by a compounding referral base
and are **not** a benchmark for paid media run cold. **A flattering ROAS on a
business with live word of mouth is as untrustworthy as a terrible one on a
business with broken attribution.**

## 14. You might not be the demo — the limit on your own judgement

**Source:** `npi7UeOE_0o`

The newest and most directly applicable limit on Rex himself, because Rex's
whole job involves rating creative he was never the audience for.

> "making sure that we're judging appropriately based on whether we are the
> avatar for the ad"

He interrupts his own Super Bowl ad ranking to make the point: if you do not own
a business you will not care about the Ramp ad at all, and that reaction says
nothing about the ad. **Then he applies the discipline to himself twice** —
hedging his verdict on the Hims & Hers ad because he has never been overweight,
and noting the Nike ad targets women and he is not one. He still issues grades;
what he withholds is confidence.

**The rule: a reaction from outside the target population carries no information
about whether the ad works.**

Two companions from the same video sharpen it into something operational:

- **The six-factor rubric is scored against the ad's objective, not absolutely.**
  He grades on attention, care, dream, urgency, social proof and what to do next
  — then knocks the Google Gemini spot for having no urgency and in the same
  breath says urgency would not be on brand for them. He names three legitimate
  objectives: pure branding, reinforcing an existing position, and direct
  response. **Never score an ad against a factor its objective does not claim.**
- **Advertising and persuasion are different outcomes.** Letting people know
  about your stuff is not the same as changing what they do, and an ad in a
  sympathetic category will always win the first. So positive sentiment is not
  evidence of a working ad.

And the escape from all of it, which he states plainly: test rather than argue.
He is explicit that his own tier grades are opinion and that data would overrule
them.

**Rex's rule: when the client is the avatar and Rex is not, the client's read on
creative outranks Rex's — and a cheap test outranks both.**

## What is NOT on this card

The gaps matter as much as the entries, because Rex must not manufacture a limit

- **No stated limit on the Core Four itself.** He treats the four as exhaustive
  and universal, and the only qualifier anywhere is that it audits which box is
  empty rather than which box is right for you — plus a sequencing rule on
  `VBoRLJimVzc` that for your first million you pick **one** of the four.
  *(`neTSqOAMgao`'s six acquisition channels is not a contradiction: the Core
  Four are ways to advertise, and referrals/word of mouth enters the earlier
  model as a lead getter — customers running the Core Four for you. Checked, and
  it reconciles.)*
- **No revenue-stage floor.** Nothing says "below $X a month, ignore this."
  There are now explicit stage **bands** above — the $1M-$3M focus expiry (§6)
  and the $1-3M "swamp" where the profit cannot afford the one hire that would
  unlock the next level (`uYds0zcAFWM`) — but still no floor below which the
  method is said not to work.
- ~~No stated B2B / B2C boundary.~~ **This has changed, and it is now one of the
  sharpest lines on the card.** He scopes the virality killers to B2B by name
  (§3), scopes demonstrate-expertise-in-advance to B2B by name (§10), and says
  virality is typically more on the consumer side. **Rex should now ask which

- **No threshold for "dead" virality.** §3 gives two conditions but no test you

## How Rex uses this card

1. **Run §1 before anything.** If the client cannot absorb double the demand,
   say so and stop. Prescribing marketing to a supply-constrained business makes
   its real problem worse.
2. **Check §2 before any paid-acquisition or back-end prescription.** The ticket
   size decides whether the monetization doctrine is a diagnosis or a dead end.
3. **Run §3's two tests before prescribing referrals or word of mouth to any
   B2B client.** Do the buyers compete with each other? Do they talk to each
   other? Two noes means the prescription is retention, not a better ask — and
   §4 says three of his scale triggers stop working at the same moment.
4. **Score the three functions before quoting any LTGP:CAC number (§5).** For a
   manual service business, 3:1 is off by a factor of five and authorises a
   spend level that will not survive the first three hires.
5. **Check §13 before quoting any ratio at all.** A number nobody can trust is
   not a weak input, it is not an input — and a flattering ROAS on a business
   with live word of mouth is just as untrustworthy.
6. **Check §14 before rating anyone's creative.** If Rex is not the avatar,
   Rex's reaction carries no information. Say so, and propose the test.
7. **Never use this card to excuse a client from work.** His standing answer to
   "that doesn't apply to me" is that it does — see the trap at the top. A limit
   is only live when he drew it or his own evidence contradicted him. The §3
   exception is available **after** running the test, never before.
8. **Say which kind of limit you are invoking:** *he states this boundary*, or
   *his own evidence contradicts his rule*, or *this is my inference.* The three
   carry different weight and the client is entitled to know which one they got.

## Rex's read for our three clients

### industries — the Denver welding shop

**This client's advice changed more than any other, and the change is about
referrals.** The dossier lists "whether anyone has ever asked a satisfied
customer for a referral on purpose" among the unknowns to fill, and treats a
foreman's referral as outperforming any ad. **§3 says that instinct needs a test
run before it becomes a plan** — and it is a trade B2B business, which is the
exact shape he scopes the virality killers to.

**First, the error Rex must not make.** The aerotech metal shop in
`uYds0zcAFWM` is *not* an analogue for this client in the way it looks. In his
telling the metal shop is the **customer** who will not refer; the business
being advised is the vendor selling into it. Reading "the welding shop is the
aerotech shop" backwards would be a misreading of the source. What transfers is
the **test**, not the character.

**Run his test on this client's own buyers**, and it splits:

| Buyer segment | Disincentive to share? | Communication density? | Referral prognosis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Competing general contractors bidding the same jobs | **Yes** — a GC who found a fast, reliable welder before a deadline has a real reason not to hand him to a rival bidder | High (the trade talks) | **Weak.** This is the aerotech mechanism |
| Plant and facilities managers, equipment owners | **No** — they do not compete with each other or with the GCs | Moderate to high | **Live** |
| Non-competing trades and suppliers | **No** | High | **Live** |

**So the correct advice is neither "ask for referrals" nor "referrals are dead"
— it is that the ask must be segmented**, and the segment split is a thing to
measure rather than assume. **This is Rex's inference**, applying his stated
test to a buyer mix he never discussed.

**And one fact settles the bigger question decisively.** The dossier records
that this business has historically run on word of mouth. **That is direct
evidence that virality is not dead here** — his test asks whether word of mouth
will appear at all, and it already has. The §3 finding therefore does *not*
write off referrals for this client. It reshapes the ask and adds a second
priority that outranks it.

**That second priority is the real headline.** In the same passage he says
retention outranks virality, B2B or B2C, full stop. The dossier's question 3 —
*average job value, and whether customers return* — is currently unanswered and
should be **promoted to the top of the list**, above the referral question. For
a business with few, high-value, reactive buyers, the twelve-month revenue
retention number is the one that decides everything else. **Ask it first.**

**§5 is the new gate before any spend conversation, and it is severe.** This is
a fully manual business on all three of his axes — manual outbound, one-to-one
sales over the phone, hands-on delivery on a fabrication floor. *(Rex's
classification, from the dossier; he never scored this business.)* That puts the
required LTGP:CAC at **20:1, floor 15:1** — not 3:1. And at a single location
with economics anywhere near 5:1-8:1, his prescription is explicit: **do not
scale, tinker.** Stay at one location and fix the model. That is a very
different conversation from "let's find a budget."

**What survives unchanged from §1.** The capacity question is still gate zero,
and the dossier does **not** record its answer — there is no capacity question
in its list of six. **That is itself the finding:** add it as question zero.
Everything else waits on it. If capacity is the binding constraint, the four
supply moves are the menu, and **raise the price** is the only one needing no
new capacity — which §5 reinforces, because price is also the fastest route to a
ratio worth scaling.

**Two smaller things.** §10's happy side still lands: a shop that has done the
work for years has an earned story that needs telling, not earning — and
`neTSqOAMgao` now converts that into the actual B2B ad strategy, *demonstrating
expertise in advance* by naming the specific failures this buyer will hit.
**And §12's warning applies with unusual force here**: the dossier says the
shop's own capacity is the marketing constraint, so a content plan requiring
weekly output from a busy floor is out of scope before it is out of budget.

### turf-monster

**The low-ticket exception (§2) is still the headline, and §3 now strengthens
rather than threatens it.** §2 routes low-ticket businesses to word of mouth and
affiliates — and §3 is where that escape hatch can be closed. **It is not closed
here.** He says virality is typically more on the consumer side, and gives the
positive case directly: nobody hides a good soda or a shirt that fits, and those
customers talk to similar people often. A consumer pick'em product passes both
of his tests — no disincentive (players do not compete for supply) and high
density (they already talk about the games). **So the ask-for-the-share mechanic
and affiliate recruitment still outrank any ad budget conversation**, and
`qsXxckCbci0` still supplies the measurable target: one acquired user bringing
at least 1.1 more.

**What §3 adds is a warning about attribution.** Referral compounding starts
only when the product is genuinely remark-worthy; a merely good product produces
linear referrals. So a flat referral rate is a verdict on the product, not a
reason to redesign the share button.

**§13 gained a second edge that cuts the other way.** With nothing reading
performance back from X or TikTok, we still have no numbers to defend a spend
decision — instrumenting comes before optimizing. But `neTSqOAMgao` adds that
**paid ads function as retargeting**: once any word of mouth exists, cold paid
traffic is not cold, and paid gets credited with demand it did not create. So
when we do instrument, the first paid numbers will read better than they are.
His own 36:1 and 100:1 figures are the warning — produced by a compounding
referral base, explicitly not a benchmark for cold paid media.

**§12 is the one that bites hardest on current practice.** The X/TikTok posting
is not automatically a funnel — entertainment audiences do not become buyers at
a rate you can build on, so the question for each post is which audience it
recruits. **And the give-in-content / ask-in-ads split is unavailable to us**,
because it requires a paid budget to carry the asks and we have none. The
corpus's own alternative is the better fit: `qpQvdBFW_yI`'s bring-a-friend
campaign put the ask *inside* the content, relentlessly, and lifted word of
mouth to 25-30%.

**One new compliance limit, and it lands squarely on this product.** The seven
ways to use "free" (`pxVeOkOVr2w`) all carry compliance stipulations — he notes
states differ and **a giveaway must not become gambling.** For a contest product
that is not a footnote; it is a precondition, and it is the one place in this
directory where his advice touches our legal surface. Treat it as a reason to
ask counsel, not as legal advice from a marketing card.

The crypto-wallet onboarding step remains the one place with **no doctrine** —
the corpus still contains nothing on onboarding friction, so treating that step
as a conversion tax is **Rex's inference, to be settled by our own funnel data.**

### operator-brand

**A strong story with no audience is still the one configuration where his
advice applies almost without reservation**, and §11 is still the trap in it.
§10's second half is exactly us: an earned, untold story needs telling, not
earning. The dietitian case (`0S5xsICW8qg`) is still the model — deep expertise,
narrow domain, tiny audience, real money — with its stated limit that it fails
if we are selling a mass-market product where reach is the constraint.

The hazard is §11: our message being *true* is not evidence anyone will buy it.
His own true fitness message sold nothing and he had to sell detoxes first. So
we lead with what a buyer will currently accept and move the real argument to
after the first result. And §7 stands over all of it — we can copy anyone's
*skills*, and we copy no one's story, least of all his.

**Two additions from the new material.**

**§6 gives the focus doctrine an expiry we are nowhere near.** One product, one
avatar, one channel holds to somewhere in the $1M-$3M band. We are far below it,
so the rule applies in full and **the instinct to open a second channel is the
thing to resist.** His diagnosis for a stalled single channel is a business or
pricing problem, not a channel problem.

**§14 is a standing limit on Rex advising this client at all.** When the avatar
is a buyer Rex is not, Rex's read on creative carries no information — and he
applies that discipline to himself twice on camera rather than only preaching
it. For this client that means the operator's own read outranks Rex's, and a
cheap test outranks both.
